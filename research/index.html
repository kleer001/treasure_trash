Competitive research · three games in the wild

Garbage Night

One raccoon simulator that television made famous and nobody kept playing, and two block-pushers that no journalist ever launched and that are still selling years later. What caused the attention, and what it was worth.

Read 2026-08-18 Sources Steam · SteamSpy · itch · YouTube · Reddit · press Subjects 3 Reviews read 39
00 — Thesis

Push once, or pull forever

Every channel that worked for one of these games worked once. Every channel that worked for the other two is still working.

Trash Panda was told about. A CTV News segment in May 2021 made a prototype viral — blogTO's own piece says "thousands have shown interest in the game since it was featured by CTV on Monday" — and CBC, Daily Hive and the city-lifestyle blogs relayed it twice, at prototype and at launch. That is push: somebody with a broadcast platform decides to tell people, reaches their audience once, and is gone.

Stephen's Sausage Roll and Baba Is You were adopted. Two famous developers vouched for one; a jam build given away free won festival awards for the other. That is pull: a small, dense, opinionated genre community takes a game up and keeps taking it up for a decade.

The load-bearing number

The single most-watched video about each game: for the raccoon simulator it is a newspaper doing news, 32k views. For the other two it is a critic's design essay — 625k and 1.9m, in both cases outperforming the developer's own trailer. In this genre, marketing is done by people explaining your game to each other.

02 — Case one

Trash Panda

Jason Leaver, solo. A 3D chill raccoon simulator on real Toronto streets, built from OpenStreetMap data. Steam appid 1669320.

Steam reviews4027 pos / 13 neg
RatingMixed67% of English reviews
Owners0 – 20klowest SteamSpy band
Concurrent1at the read date
Median playtime0.8 h21 of 39 under one hour

You are a raccoon on a Toronto street — real streets, real mailboxes and bus stops, named real neighbourhoods. You knock over bins, eat what spills, score points for mess. There are power-ups, a garbage truck on a fixed patrol route, and rival raccoons you can challenge to a bin-knocking race.

No fail state, no timer, no level structure, no story, no puzzle. The store blurb, verbatim: "A small, chill, low-fi raccoon simulator, where you play as a raccoon roaming neighbourhoods of Toronto making a mess."

Timeline

2020–21Built during lockdown by a filmmaker with no prior game credits, after raccoons raided his green bin.
2021-05-12Prototype goes viral after a CTV News feature. blogTO: "People are obsessed with this Toronto raccoon video game and it hasn't even launched yet."
2022-02Steam Next Fest — demo and developer stream.
2023-11-15Launch, $9.99, with an in-person party in Toronto.
2024-04Busiest month: new camera, compass pointers ("requested many times for a very long time"), a new largest neighbourhood, and a brand collab with meal-kit company Goodfood — a promo skin and a porch-pirate mechanic.
2024-06-03Last update of any kind.
2024-08-14Board thread: "Abandoned by dev?" — zero replies.
2025-11-08Board thread: "Saving?" — zero replies.

Eighteen discussion threads, zero developer replies on any of them — including direct feature requests and "is anyone home". The niche has an incumbent who is not defending it.

The one-liners are almost all positive — "Raccoon.", "this is literally me.", "racoons are cool, dude." The 14 reviews long enough to carry an argument split exactly 7 positive to 7 negative, and seven of those fourteen use the word "potential". Nobody who used it meant it kindly.

The single finding

There's no actual game here at all, no explanation of what to do, no story, no meaningful goals. The only thing there is to do is to knock bins over and eat garbage. I know, that sounds great, but it gets old in about 30 seconds.

Negative · 0.2 hours played

Nice idea and I like the vibe, but sadly the game lacks any depth at all… the core mechanic is basically discovered and mastered within the first few minutes. Then it's just grinding to tip over all the bins.

Negative · 10.7 hours played — the longest session in the set

Definitely add some missions or more activities also.

Positive · 1.6 hours played

What they praised — the part worth taking seriously

The praise is almost never about content. It is about the physical comedy of one verb: strike the container, watch the contents scatter.

The physics of the individual pieces of trash is good, and probably one of the most entertaining things about this game… a raccoon yeeting a full sized herby curby across a street in a single slap is entertaining.

Negative · 4.3 hours · the most-upvoted review in the set, titled "More Potential than Game"

That verb carried a structureless game to 67% positive with a broken camera on top of it. Every unit of praise here is about the moment; every complaint is about what surrounds it.

The price lesson

Six reviews mention money, five negatively, and the recurring verdict is "get it on sale" or "you are simply donating". $9.99 set the expectation of a game and what shipped was an experience. Stephen's Sausage Roll charged three times as much and was praised for the nerve. Price is not high or low — it is congruent or incongruent with what the thing turns out to be.

04 — Case one

Where the attention actually came from

Not whether it got attention — it got two waves — but which channel produced it, whether that channel repeats, and whether it reached people who buy games.

Television — the only thing that worked

CBC ran a video segment. CTV News Toronto ran a piece. CFRB ran a radio interview. A TV producer chose this story because it was about Toronto and raccoons, not because it was a good game — a channel you cannot plan, cannot repeat and cannot aim, delivering an audience of local news viewers.

Reddit — did not happen

r/toronto, all time, top-sorted: one relevant post — a link to the blogTO article, 41 votes and 8 comments. A whole-of-Reddit search returns nothing on topic. For a game whose premise is a running local joke in a city with a 498,000-member subreddit, this is the striking number in the file. The story was told at Torontonians by television; it was never taken up by them.

05 — Case two

Stephen's Sausage Roll

The control case: a block-pusher with no marketing at all that outsold the raccoon simulator by an order of magnitude at three times the price, and is still selling a decade later.

Steam reviews1,580Overwhelmingly Positive

Price$29.99never a budget title

The store page, in full

A simple 3d puzzle game.

The entire Steam description

Six words. No feature bullets, no pillars, no adjectives. The lesson is not "write less" — it is that copy was not the variable. What sold this game happened entirely outside the store page.

Two developers vouching

Before release, Jonathan Blow (Braid, The Witness) and Bennett Foddy (QWOP, Getting Over It) both praised it publicly.

One of the best puzzle games ever made, or one of the best video games ever made.

After this game comes out, if your game design school isn't teaching it, then it is just a bad school.

Jonathan Blow

That is the whole launch campaign. Two people the target audience already trusted said it was important, and a small, dense, well-connected audience moved. One let's-play in the search results is literally titled "Puzzle Game Recommended by Jonathan Blow!"

Game Grumps found it seven years after launch and brought 600k+ views in two videos; there was no launch window to miss. And someone published a fresh state-space analysis within the past year that 64,000 people watched — a decade on, the game is still generating explanation.

The move

The free build was the marketing. Not a demo carved out of a finished game to drive wishlists — the whole idea, given away, two years early, where anyone could play it and anyone could judge it. Every award above was won by something nobody had paid for.

Why the rules-as-blocks design is itself a marketing asset: the mechanic is legible in a screenshot and astonishing in a GIF. A player who breaks it in a funny way has something to post; an essayist has something to explain. Both market the game for free, forever. That property is designed in, not bolted on.

The honest caveat. Baba Is You is a generational outlier — an original mechanic that had not existed before, from a developer who won a major jam on the first try. Nothing here is a formula that reproduces the outcome. What reproduces is the order of operations: free build in public → festival validation → platform attention → paid release → permanent presence. That order is available to anyone. The result is not.

The shelf, and the field below it

The block-pusher audience is small, extremely literate about the genre, and unforgiving about level design.

This genre does not produce Mixed ratings. Every entry is Very or Overwhelmingly Positive. Puzzle players either accept a game or ignore it; a weak puzzle game does not get a Mixed rating, it gets no reviews at all.

But the floor is thin. Sokobond Express has 107 reviews with a long-pedigree developer behind it. Below about $15 and outside the Draknek orbit the tail falls off fast. Nobody on this shelf got there on theme — they got there on the quality of the levels.

The irreversibility corner

  • A Monster's Expedition (Draknek, 2020) — push trees, they fall and become permanent bridges. Terrain created by acting, then routed around. Closest published relative to "your actions rewrite the floor", except its changes are usually enabling rather than disabling.
  • Bonfire Peaks (Corey Martin / Draknek, 2021) — burn your own belongings. Consumption as goal rather than obstacle, same register: the board is a thing you use up.
  • Classic Sokoban (1982) — the origin of irreversible deadlock. You cannot pull, so a wrong push is permanent. There it is a failure mode, not the subject.
  • Bomb-Sokoban variants — the direct inverse. Bombs clear space rather than closing it.

Nothing on that list is themed. Logs, crates, atoms, trains. The shelf is aggressively abstract — which is both the opening and the risk.

The free browser field

itch.io, tags puzzle + sokoban: 2,297 results. Sampling the front of that list, the overwhelming majority are free, browser-playable and jam-scale. Two consequences: discovery is the binding constraint, not quality — being better than the median sokoban on itch is not a plan. And nothing in that field is wearing the raccoon costume; the closest thing to a mascot in the sampled front rank was a pug.

08 — Synthesis

Four patterns that repeat

In this genre, the top video is an essay

Baba's most-watched video is Game Maker's Toolkit explaining the design (1.9m). Sausage Roll's is Joseph Anderson explaining the design (625k) — 2.6× the developer's own trailer. Trash Panda's is a newspaper's news segment. Puzzle games are marketed by people explaining them, which makes legibility a growth mechanic: a rule you can state in one sentence and demonstrate in a five-second clip recruits critics, and critics have audiences you cannot buy.

There is no launch window

Game Grumps picked up Sausage Roll seven years after release and delivered 600k+ views. It still collects Steam reviews at ten years. Baba's itch page is still rated and still updated in 2026. Trash Panda had its entire life in its launch month, twice, because news is an event and not a relationship. A puzzle game that is good is never late, and effort spent on a launch spike is misallocated.

The free public artifact is the marketing

Baba's jam build was free, permanent, and won the awards that made everything after it possible. It was not a funnel or a cut-down demo — it was the idea, given away, where anyone including festival judges could reach it.

Presence compounds, and so does absence

Baba: devlogs through 2026, level editor, merch, vinyl, seven years of visible life. Trash Panda: silent since June 2024, eighteen threads, zero replies, one titled "Abandoned by dev?". The audience each one has today is the sum of those decisions.

Asks stated inside reviews, where nobody answers either

  • "Definitely add some missions or more activities also."
  • "it just needs some TLC (and some objectives/goals) to really capture people."
  • "Things I wish it had: a full map you can view to see what ro[ads/bins you've missed]…"
  • "it would be cool if you had to avoid moving cars too"

Before anyone answers one of these. Answering a lone voice is done on its merits and verified first, and posting publicly is a decision for whoever owns the account. Nothing here has been posted anywhere. A self-promotional drive-by on a dead board is worse than silence — and there is a real person at the other end of that silence.

11 — Conclusion

The gap

Findings, not instructions. Inferences are labelled as inferences.

The costume was proven and the game under it was never built; the shelf is full of excellent games wearing no costume at all. The crossing is unoccupied in both directions — the raccoon games have no puzzle, and the puzzles have no raccoon.

What would not be a compliment

Half of the raccoon simulator's negative reviews are 3D-open-world failures: camera collision, terrain that fails to load, invisible walls, empty maps, achievements pointing at unbuilt levels. A one-screen deterministic grid does not have those problems to have. Beating it on those axes is a property of choosing a different format, not an accomplishment. The contested axis is level design, and the competition there is Draknek's catalogue.

Risks the evidence surfaces

  • The theme could repel the shelf. The block-pusher audience is abstract-leaning and has seen a thousand cute Sokoban reskins. A mascot reads as a jam game to exactly the people most qualified to appreciate the rooms.
  • The fantasy was Toronto's, not the raccoon's. Inference: a meaningful share of that press was civic — a local joke made playable, not inheritable by anything set nowhere in particular.
  • The browser field is 2,297 competitors deep. Discovery, not quality, binds.
  • A costume writes a cheque the rooms must cash. That audience arrived on charm and left over substance. A puzzle game that leads with charm and has thirty mediocre rooms fails the same way, one shelf over.

What the evidence cannot help with

Every game on the shelf is there because its levels are good. No competitor analysis, positioning line or channel list moves that number.

The single sentence

Trash Panda was told about by broadcasters and never adopted by anyone; the two exemplars were adopted by a small, dense, opinionated genre community and then spent years compounding — so the transferable move is not to seek coverage, it is to put a free, finished, legible thing in front of the people who argue about puzzle design, and then still be there in three years.
