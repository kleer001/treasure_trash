# What the three cases add up to

Findings, not instructions. Everything here is an inference from the market evidence in the
other files, and where something is a judgement rather than a measured fact it is labelled. The
game these findings were gathered for is *Treasure Trash* — [`the-game.md`](./the-game.md) — and
naming it changes nothing about what the evidence says; it just means the last section has
somebody in it.

## The thesis in one sentence

*Trash Panda* proved the costume and never built a game under it; the block-pusher shelf is full
of excellent games wearing no costume at all; the crossing of the two is currently unoccupied in
both directions.

## What the raccoon simulator did right

**1. The verb was the product.** Every piece of praise in 39 Steam reviews is about the physical
comedy of one action: strike the container, watch the contents fly. Nobody praised a level, a
system or a map. That single verb carried a structureless game to 67% positive with a broken
camera.

**2. The fantasy is legible in four words.** "You are a raccoon knocking over garbage." No
trailer needed, no genre knowledge assumed, and it put a first-time developer on CBC and CTV
twice. Compare the shelf: "recursive box containment", "push logs to make bridges", "molecular
bonding" — excellent games that each require a paragraph.

**3. Solo, personal, present.** The launch announcements read like a person rather than a studio.
At this scale that is worth more than polish, and it was working right up until it stopped.

## What it did wrong

**1. No goal.** The central, repeated, unanimous complaint. Nothing to be good at, so the
fantasy ran out in the time it takes to knock over ten bins.

**2. Mastery in five minutes.** A reviewer with 10.7 hours logged: "the core mechanic is
basically discovered and mastered within the first few minutes." The block-pusher shelf is built
on the opposite curve — the mechanic is understood in one room and still generating new problems
thirty rooms later.

**3. $10 for an experience.** Five of six money-mentioning reviews say wait for a sale or call
it a donation. Price is not high or low; it is congruent or incongruent with what the thing
turns out to be. Stephen's Sausage Roll charged three times as much and was praised for it.

**4. Silence.** Last update June 2024, eighteen board threads, zero developer replies, one
titled "Abandoned by dev?". *Inference:* a devlog cadence and answered comments would have held
that audience for very little work. It is the failure that is cheapest to avoid and easiest to
repeat.

## Where "better than it" would not be a compliment

Half of that game's negative reviews are 3D-open-world failures — camera collision, terrain that
fails to load, invisible walls, empty maps, achievements pointing at unbuilt levels. A
deterministic one-screen grid does not have those problems to have. Beating it on those axes is
a property of the format, not an accomplishment.

The axis that is genuinely contested is level design, and the competition there is Draknek's
catalogue, not a lone raccoon sim.

## Risks the evidence surfaces for the crossing

- **The theme could repel the shelf.** The block-pusher audience is abstract-leaning and has
  seen a thousand cute reskins of Sokoban on itch. A mascot reads as a jam game to exactly the
  people most qualified to appreciate the rooms. The counter is to lead with the mechanic in
  motion rather than the mascot.
- **The fantasy was Toronto's, not the raccoon's.** *Inference:* a meaningful share of that
  game's press and goodwill was civic — a local joke made playable. That is not inheritable by
  anything set nowhere in particular. Whatever replaces it has to come from the mechanic.
- **The free browser field is 2,297 competitors deep.** Discovery, not quality, is the binding
  constraint.
- **A costume writes a cheque the rooms must cash.** That audience arrived on charm and left
  over substance. A puzzle game that leads with charm and has thirty mediocre rooms fails the
  same way, one shelf over.

## The positioning the evidence would support

Observations about what is available to say. Not copy — nobody has written any yet.

- **The available true difference:** in most block-pushers the mess is something you fix. A
  mechanic where mess is the only thing you make, never goes away, and has to be routed through
  is genuinely uncontested on this shelf.
- **The fair comparable:** the log-pushing of *A Monster's Expedition*, except the terrain you
  create is always in your way.
- **The inversion players already know:** bomb-Sokoban clears space; an additive mechanic closes
  it.
- **The asset that would sell it:** one clip, under fifteen seconds, where a mistake and its
  consequence are both legible without words. Not the genre name, not a level count, not tech.

## Where Treasure Trash sits in this

Encouraging, and honest about which parts are still promises.

**It is standing in the empty square.** A raccoon, garbage, and a real Sokoban-family engine with
a solver and verified pars behind it. Both halves of the crossing, in one page, and the
`competitive-landscape.md` sweep found nothing else wearing both.

**The complaint that sank the incumbent is this game's subject.** "Nothing to do" is what 39
reviews say in one voice. A room with a par is the answer, and there are 61 of them already.

**Its true difference is the one the shelf has not got.** The mess is the only thing you make and
it never comes up again — the bomb-Sokoban inversion, above, is available to say honestly because
it is what the engine does.

**The risks land softly.** "The theme could repel the shelf" is answered by leading with the
mechanic in motion, which costs one clip. "The fantasy was Toronto's" is a reason not to inherit
someone else's civic joke, and the alley at 3 a.m. is nowhere in particular on purpose.
"Discovery binds" is true and the doors are all listed and open.

**And the cheque is the honest part.** A costume writes one the rooms must cash. That is not a
warning about a mistake already made — it is a description of the work in progress, on a game
that has shipped nothing, told nobody, and can therefore still spend as long on the rooms as they
turn out to need.

## The thing this research cannot help with

Every game on the shelf in `competitive-landscape.md` is there because its levels are good. No
competitor analysis, positioning line or channel list moves that number. It is the one number
that is entirely in the developer's hands, which is the good news and the whole job at once.
