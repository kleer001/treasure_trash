Block-Pusher Laws

Ground rules for Treasure Trash — an untimed, Sokoban-family command puzzle with a verb-based upgrade tree.

The through-line every list descends from: a block-pusher is a contract of pure, visible logic. Every “surprise” must be the player discovering they under-read the logic — never the game changing it on them. This is Jonathan Blow’s “surprise = information content,” Sokoban’s fair deadlock, and The Witness’s teach-in-isolation, in one sentence.

1The Laws — unbreakable contracts

Break these and the player feels cheated, not challenged.

  1. Determinism. Same inputs → same result, always. No RNG in resolution. ArchivistSuperfan
  2. Full information. Everything needed to solve is on screen. Lolo shows the whole room at once; no off-screen state decides the answer. Superfan
  3. No execution tax. With the clock gone, solving is thinking, never doing. A solution you’ve figured out can always be input. Superfan
  4. Failure is legible and free to undo. A soft-lock whose cause you can see is a puzzle; one you can’t diagnose is a bug. Sokoban’s corner-lock is always visible. CriticShipper
  5. One consistent rule, everywhere. A block pushes the same in room 1 and room 50. Consistency is the substrate that makes deduction possible. Archivist
  6. Unambiguous goal. The player always knows what “solved” means (all shiny → the hoard). Superfan
  7. Foreseeable dead-ends. You can reason “this strands me” before committing — the tell is visible. A lock knowable only in hindsight is a trap. Critic
  8. Guaranteed solvable. Every shipped room is actually solvable — and you can prove it (see List 4). Shipper

2The Laws of Breaking Laws — the deepening “aha”

How to make a player find a law was deeper/stranger — while staying perfectly logical. This is the Blow zone, and the Critic’s home turf.

  1. Break assumptions, never rules. The player assumed a limit that was never stated. You reveal the true rule was always broader. Their model changes; the system doesn’t. Critic
  2. Retroactively obvious. After the aha: “of course — it was always true, I just never tried.” If it feels bolted on, you cheated. Critic
  3. Teach the twist in isolation, then combine (The Witness’s method): a safe room where the deeper truth is unmistakable, then stack it on old mechanics. Archivist
  4. Let the wrong assumption fail cheaply and legibly. The obvious move soft-locks visibly, and the failure itself hints at the deeper rule. Free undo makes this a teacher, not a punishment. SuperfanShipper
  5. One reinterpretation per room. Two “actually it’s stranger” reveals at once and the player can’t attribute the lesson. Superfan
  6. The strange truth must generalize. A one-off is a gimmick; a deeper law that re-colors every prior room is elegance. CriticArchivist
  7. Interaction, never exposition. No text box explains the twist. They do it and see it. Archivist

Three drafted for Treasure Trash — all legal by rule 1 (the elements were always visible; only the assumption hid the truth):

  • Assume the crow can’t cross tall trash. Truth: she can perch on a spilled stack and hop the gap — she was never grounded, you just never tried perching.
  • Assume opening always fills space (a hindrance). Truth: sometimes you want to spill trash to bridge water or wall off a rival — the hindrance was always a tool.
  • Assume the two must be kept apart (bad). Truth: a controlled bump shoves the other exactly one tile — deliberate collision as a nudge tool. The antagonism was always usable. The Critic’s keystone: this turns “keep apart” into “controlled proximity is a tool,” so the odd-couple theme finally lives in the rules, not the art.

3Conventions that make play fun — not generic juice

Structural conventions specific to the genre. The Superfan owns this list.

  1. Free, instant undo + restart. The most important thing in the genre; it converts soft-locks from punishment into experimentation. (Baba Is You, Snakebird, Stephen’s Sausage Roll all lean on it.)
  2. Single-screen, glanceable rooms. One puzzle, fully visible, no camera hunting. Archivist
  3. “Solve on paper.” A good room is solvable by looking, before touching; movement just executes the plan.
  4. One-new-idea cadence: introduce → complicate → combine → subvert. Every room has a point. Archivist
  5. Elegance — fewest parts, richest interaction. Cut any element not load-bearing for the solution. Three interacting beats ten inert. Critic
  6. The “I’m brilliant” payoff. The aha is the reward — the loot is just the receipt.
  7. Difficulty by depth, never obscurity. Hard because the interaction is deep, not because a rule is hidden or fiddly. Critic
  8. Optional mastery layer (par-command, bonus loot) that never gates progress.
  9. Bite-sized rooms, generous checkpoints — frequent small victories. Shipper

4Anything else to keep in mind

  • Ship a solver in the test suite. Law 1.8 (guaranteed solvable) is load-bearing, and the house rule is “new behavior gets a test.” A brute-force solver that proves each room is solvable and surfaces unintended solutions is the highest-value thing to build early. Shipper
  • Two movers doubles the state space. Sim-legibility is gone, but a new combinatorial legibility risk appears — the player holds two units’ possibilities in mind. Keep early rooms small. Shipper
  • Reversibility is a tuning knob. Sokoban is 100% irreversible (hence undo-reliance). We choose the mix — moving animals reversible, opening irreversible. More irreversibility = harder, more soft-locky. Critic
  • Enemies as logic, not reflexes — Lolo’s real lesson. Medusa is a line-of-sight rule; Snakey is a push-a-block-onto-it rule. Any rival/hazard must be a deterministic logic piece, never a twitch threat. Archivist
  • The “just Sokoban” trap. If the game reskins to warehouse crates with nothing lost, the crow/raccoon is decoration. The differentiators that must earn their keep: asymmetric movers (flyer vs. shover), two appetites, and opening reshapes terrain — plus the controlled-bump reveal. CriticArchivist

The one conflict worth naming — Critic vs. Superfan. The Critic wants assumption-breaking reveals (List 2); the Superfan guards full information (Law 1.2). They look opposed but aren’t: the info was always visible; only the player’s untested assumption hid it. A reveal leaning on off-screen state violates the Superfan; a reveal about an untested affordance of a visible element satisfies both. Visible affordance, yes; hidden state, no — that’s the line to design on.

+Addendum — Mutations: block-pushers with upgrades & progression

Yes — the genre mutates, and several games graft progression onto the pure-logic core without breaking the Laws above. The whole trick is what you let the player upgrade. There are three safe flavors and one that fights the genre.

Flavor A — Verb / tool acquisition (a tree of keys, not stats)

You gain new discrete, fully-legible abilities; rooms that were unsolvable now open. The “tree” is a tree of verbs. This is the safest and oldest form.

  • The Legend of Zelda — dungeon items are the skill tree: each item is a key that makes a new class of puzzle solvable (hookshot, bombs, boomerang).
  • Adventures of Lolo — per-screen powers (build a bridge, smash rock): the ability defines the room. Our direct ancestor already does this. Archivist
  • Toki Tori 2 — two abilities (stomp, tweet), combined in ever-deeper ways. Proof that a tiny verb-set + growth-by-combination beats a big stat sheet.
  • The Talos Principle — accreting deterministic tools (jammers, connectors, boxes) that recombine.

Flavor B — Loadout / unit unlocks (cut)

Removed from the corpus. This flavor rested entirely on a squad-tactics game with meta-progression, and Treasure Trash is not that: one animal, one screen, no run structure, no loadout chosen before a room. Reasoning from it kept pulling the design toward a game we are not making. If a deterministic-options progression is ever wanted here, it needs a precedent from this genre — a single-avatar, single-screen block-pusher — not a tactics game borrowed for its determinism.

Flavor C — Knowledge-gating (“metroidbrainia”)

The upgrade is in the player’s head — no stats at all. Progress = understanding. This is just List 2 (breaking laws) scaled to a whole game.

  • A Monster’s Expedition — an open world of Sokoban islands, gated only by what you’ve figured out.
  • Toki Tori 2 & Baba Is You — no formal unlocks; the barrier is your own ignorance. (See the “metroidbrainia” concept.) Critic

The one flavor to avoid — stat-creep trees. “+1 push strength,” “+10% speed,” “unlock 3rd move.” Continuous or hidden power lets a room be beaten by grinding instead of thinking, and it breaks Law 1.2 (full information) and the “solve on paper” convention. Puzzle Quest gets away with an RPG skill tree only because it’s a match-3 (an execution + RNG genre), not a pure-logic puzzle. Don’t bolt a stat sheet onto a logic puzzle. CriticSuperfan

Takeaway for Treasure Trash. Our equips are the skill tree — a tree of verbs (Iron Claws, Nimble Paws, perch-on-trash, the controlled bump…), chosen before a room and fully known inside it. Model it on Lolo’s per-screen powers: every node is a new discrete, deterministic ability that opens new rooms — never a stat multiplier. And some of the “tree” can be pure knowledge-gating (a List 2 reveal is an upgrade, in your head). That gives you the progression you want while keeping all eight Laws intact.

References & important games

The block-pusher lineage

Sokoban — Hiroyuki Imabayashi / Thinking Rabbit, 1982 (NEC PC-8801). The genre’s root: push-only, can’t pull, and the player can strand themselves — the irreversible deadlock is the puzzle. 20 levels drawn on graph paper.

Adventures of Lolo — HAL Laboratory, 1989 (NES). Single-screen rooms; collect all hearts → chest → exit. Enemies are logic elements (Medusa = line-of-sight; Snakey = push a block onto it); magic shots turn foes into pushable, water-bridging eggs. (See also the Eggerland Wiki.)

Chip’s Challenge — Epyx, 1989. Tile-logic puzzles: keys, doors, hazards, and blocks as consistent rules.

Modern craft & the “aha”

Baba Is You — Hempuli (Arvi Teikari), 2019. Dozens of levels from one mechanic; the rules themselves are pushable blocks — the ultimate “the law was deeper than you assumed.”

The Witness — Thekla / Jonathan Blow, 2016. Teaches each rule in isolation, then combines — and never with words.

Stephen’s Sausage Roll — increpare (Stephen Lavelle), 2016. Tiny piece-set, brutal depth; a masterclass in minimum parts / maximum interaction.

Snakebird — Noumenon Games, 2015. Deceptively cute; leans entirely on free undo to make its strandable states fair.

Multi-character command (the control model)

The Lost Vikings — Silicon & Synapse (Blizzard), 1992. Multiple characters, distinct abilities, one puzzle screen — unfinishable without each one’s kit. Our asymmetric-movers ancestor.

Pikmin — Nintendo, 2001. Command creatures to targets; they act on arrival. The click-to-send verb.

Mutations — block-pushers & puzzles with progression

A Monster’s Expedition — Draknek & Friends, 2020. An open world of log-rolling Sokoban islands, gated purely by understanding.

Toki Tori 2 — Two Tribes, 2013. A puzzle metroidvania with only two abilities, deepened by combination and knowledge-gating rather than stat unlocks.

The Talos Principle — Croteam, 2014. First-person puzzles built on accreting deterministic tools that recombine.

The Legend of Zelda — Nintendo, 1986. Dungeon items as a de-facto skill tree: each is a key that unlocks a new class of puzzle.

Puzzle Quest: Challenge of the Warlords — Infinite Interactive, 2007. The archetypal puzzle + RPG skill-tree graft — and a caution: it works because it’s match-3 (execution + RNG), not pure logic.

“Metroidbrainia” — knowledge-gated games — Thinky Games (feature). On progression that lives in the player’s head, not a stat sheet.
